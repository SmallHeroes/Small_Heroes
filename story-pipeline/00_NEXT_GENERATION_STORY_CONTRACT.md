# Next-Generation Story Contract

**Status:** staging contract for new story development.

**Runtime authority:** none.

**Approved-bank effect:** none.

**Audience:** writers, premise reviewers, story editors, and later compiler authors.

This contract replaces “start with the resilience lesson and decorate it with a plot.” A Small Heroes story must work first as a story a child wants to hear again. Its emotional value is carried by action, consequence, relationship, and play.

## 1. The four-layer model

Every story must keep these layers distinct:

1. **Visible plot:** a concrete, funny problem and a child-owned want.
2. **Play engine:** repeatable physical rules, objects, attempts, and consequences.
3. **Relationship engine:** the companion's lovable flaw creates wrong help; the child can do something the companion cannot.
4. **Emotional underlayer:** the challenge category shapes choices but is not announced as the subject or lesson.

If the plot can be summarized as “the child feels X and the companion teaches Y,” the premise fails.

## 2. Locked product and format constraints

- Primary read-aloud target: ages 4–7, with child-first humor and a quiet adult reread layer.
- Child placeholder: `{{childName}}`; Hebrew gender personalization remains a technical requirement at prose time.
- Beat counts remain: bedtime 8, adventure 12, fantasy 16.
- Every eventual beat requires prose plus an English `imageDirection` in the approved bank format.
- The named child is the active hero and owns the climax.
- Companion ID, display name, gender, body, accessory, scale, and visual identity remain unchanged.
- Existing approved stories are immutable and are not creative seeds.

## 3. Premise hard gates

A premise is disqualified if any item is absent:

- a strange, funny, or irresistible event visible on page 1;
- a concrete want that belongs to the child and is not “feel calm/brave/better”;
- a physical problem that can change on the page;
- a playable system with at least one concrete object and repeatable action;
- companion wrong help caused by the companion's specific flaw;
- two materially different child attempts that fail for causal reasons;
- at least three escalating humor opportunities, including one physical failure image;
- a discovery available to the child, not delivered as advice;
- a decisive child action that changes the external problem;
- a visible release/payoff beyond understanding, breathing, or calming;
- a convincing reason a child cares and a separate reason a parent enjoys rereading;
- a clear explanation of why the idea is not a therapeutic fable.

Immediate rejection:

- resilience theme in the title or hook;
- abstract emotion as the only problem;
- magic stone/mirror/light whose only function is to represent a feeling;
- companion solves the climax;
- moral, worksheet, exposure exercise, or “three steps to cope” structure;
- old-bank plot, scene, catchphrase, or object sequence reused as a seed;
- a story that survives the companion swap test.

## 4. Page-turn and structure contract

Before prose, every beat must name:

- what physically happens;
- what the child does;
- what the companion does or gets wrong;
- what changes;
- what becomes funnier, harder, stranger, or more urgent;
- why a child wants the next page;
- the visual anchor and current set piece.

No two consecutive beats may repeat the same dramatic state. A quiet beat is valid when it changes knowledge, relationship, plan, scale, ownership, or anticipation.

The climax belongs to the child. The companion may supply access, comic pressure, a flawed model, or a tool, but cannot explain or perform the answer.

## 5. Location and visual dramaturgy

Location variety exists to change the problem, not to decorate it.

- **Bedtime:** normally at least two meaningful set pieces; a single location is allowed only if its physical rules and staging transform substantially.
- **Adventure:** normally at least three meaningful set pieces with distinct obstacles or uses of the play system.
- **Fantasy:** at least three distinct set pieces governed by one coherent world rule.
- More than three consecutive beats with the same staging requires a deliberate reason at outline review.
- A renamed corner of the same room is not a new set piece.
- Each transition must create a new choice, obstacle, joke, clue, or use of an object.

Visual richness must remain producible: recurring props are limited and stable; the story does not introduce a new crowd, costume, or hero object on every page.

## 6. Direction contracts

### Bedtime — 8 beats

Bedtime is an **energy curve**, not a location.

- May begin anywhere and may travel.
- Beats 1–5 may contain motion, surprise, pursuit, discovery, and comic escalation.
- By beats 6–8, choices narrow, rhythm lengthens, movement becomes smaller, and the external problem earns a safe closure.
- The child does not have to be in bed, indoors, sleepy, or asleep.
- The ending must lower activation without using a breathing exercise, lullaby template, or “and then everything was calm.”

### Adventure — 12 beats

- A concrete destination, retrieval, rescue, construction, delivery, contest, or mystery.
- Escalation changes the plan at least twice.
- The companion's wrong help creates meaningful trouble.
- The child owns the decisive maneuver.
- The ending pays off the quest and the relationship; it does not summarize the lesson.

### Fantasy — 16 beats

- One clear world rule that can generate many consequences.
- At least three set pieces that test the same rule differently.
- Wonder and comedy arise from causal use of the rule, not a tour of unrelated marvels.
- No abstract therapeutic world, symbolic feeling object, or epic inflation of a small companion.
- The final release is physical, surprising, and illustratable.

## 7. Companion contract

Every story must use the selected companion bible as a causal engine:

- **desire:** what the companion wants inside the visible plot;
- **flaw:** the lovable belief or habit that makes help imperfect;
- **wrong help:** the action that complicates the problem;
- **comic engine:** a repeatable setup → betrayal/mistake → consequence pattern that escalates;
- **embodied signature:** body, accessory, sound, or movement that affects action;
- **voice:** recognizable rhythm and word choice without mandatory catchphrase spam;
- **child-only ability:** what the child notices, chooses, invents, says, or dares that the companion cannot;
- **change:** what the companion learns from the child through action;
- **swap test:** replacing the companion must break plot mechanics, jokes, and climax.

A canonical accessory is not sufficient. If removing the accessory leaves the same story, the companion is still generic.

## 8. Hebrew read-aloud contract

- Write spoken Hebrew, not translated prose and not adult poetry in short sentences.
- Prefer concrete verbs, precise nouns, sound, rhythm, interruption, and playful repetition.
- Vary sentence length; short sentences are impact tools, not the default rhythm for every page.
- Dialogue must reveal desire, flaw, joke, or change. It may not deliver a therapy explanation.
- Use onomatopoeia and wordplay when they belong to the physical action or companion.
- Aim for at least two quotable lines per story: one child-repeatable line and one line an adult enjoys rereading.
- Niqqud follows the active bank convention and is used where pronunciation or ambiguity requires it; it is not decorative noise.
- One explicit resilience line is the maximum, not a requirement. Zero is preferred when action already carries the meaning.

Forbidden prose shapes:

- “הרגש שלו היה כמו…” as a substitute for action;
- “הגוף הגיע לפני הראש” and similar adult-therapeutic abstractions;
- “ואז הוא הבין ש…” as climax;
- narrator praise that labels the child brave/calm/good;
- a final moral or direct address to the reader.

## 9. Humor and reread contract

Humor must be built into causality:

- one companion-specific recurring engine;
- rule-of-three escalation with changed consequences;
- at least one strong visual failure image;
- no joke at the child's fear, body, sensitivity, identity, or mistake;
- no adult sarcasm or pop-culture wink that breaks the world;
- the parent layer comes from precision, timing, recognition, or a line with a second meaning.

Reread value requires at least two of:

- a recurring detail whose meaning changes;
- a visual clue a child can anticipate;
- a phrase that becomes funnier on repetition;
- setup/payoff across distant beats;
- a companion behavior a child can imitate in play;
- a final image that recontextualizes the opening.

## 10. Acceptance ladder

### Premise gate

Twelve diverse candidates → deterministic hard-fail review → human critique → three finalists → Guy selects one.

### Spine gate

Selected premise → complete beat map → page-turn review → location/continuity feasibility → Guy approves prose work.

### Prose gate

Full story → oral-Hebrew edit → humor/agency/swap review → deterministic bank-format validation → parent/child read-aloud evidence → Guy content acceptance.

### Integration gate

Only an accepted story receives a new versioned bank path, source hash, visual-contract rebuild, and later explicitly approved render proof. No stage inherits approval from an older story with the same slot ID.

## 11. Story-level acceptance checklist

- [ ] Page 1 contains the visible hook.
- [ ] The child's want is concrete and independent of the resilience theme.
- [ ] The child causes at least three meaningful state changes and owns the climax.
- [ ] The companion creates wrong help and is changed by the child.
- [ ] The comic engine escalates at least three times.
- [ ] Every beat changes state and earns the next page.
- [ ] Set pieces satisfy the direction contract or document a deliberate exception.
- [ ] The payoff is external, visible, and prepared by earlier beats.
- [ ] Emotional meaning remains under the plot.
- [ ] Hebrew passes a real read-aloud without repair-on-the-fly.
- [ ] At least two lines are worth repeating.
- [ ] The companion swap test fails decisively.
- [ ] The story remains safe, personalized, and illustratable.
- [ ] Guy has approved the content; independent technical review covers only implementation claims.
